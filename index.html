--- # 03 — Our Core Research Question ## The problem we are investigating
### Central Question **How can existing early-intervention and diversion pathways be strengthened to reduce repeated youth offending in NSW?**

### Supporting questions **01 — Identification** Which factors are associated with repeated youth offending? **02 — Intervention** Which diversion and early-intervention programs demonstrate measurable outcomes? **03 — Implementation** Why do apparently effective interventions produce different outcomes across young people or settings? **04 — Continuity** Where do gaps occur between diversion, family support, education, employment and longer-term community services? --- # 04 — What Already Exists? ## NSW already has intervention mechanisms ### Diversion - Police warnings and cautions - Youth Justice Conferences - Community-based responses ### Early intervention - **Youth on Track** - Case management - Family and cognitive-behavioural interventions ### Community support - Mentoring - Education and employment support - Housing and social services
### Therefore, the problem is NOT simply: **“There are no programs.”** ### Our emerging question is: **“Are existing interventions sufficiently targeted, coordinated and sustained?”**
--- # 05 — What We Have Discovered ## Early evidence points to a complex picture ### Finding 01 **Youth offending is not simply increasing.** Long-term NSW data indicates substantial declines in youth offending. --- ### Finding 02 **Reoffending remains concentrated among a smaller but important group.** Young people released from sentenced custody experience particularly high rates of subsequent offending. --- ### Finding 03 **Intervention does not automatically equal effectiveness.** Evaluation of Youth on Track found mixed outcomes, including no statistically significant overall effect on reoffending and custody outcomes. --- ### Finding 04 **Community relationships may matter.** Evidence on youth mentoring identifies factors such as matching, supervision, engagement and community networks as important components.
Sources: BOCSAR (2022; 2026); NSW Department of Communities and Justice (2022). --- # 06 — The Gap We Identified ## Our emerging intervention opportunity
Programs exist.
But pathways may not always connect.

### Potential gaps **TARGETING** Are interventions sufficiently tailored to individual needs? ↓ **COORDINATION** Are education, family, justice and community services connected? ↓ **CONTINUITY** What happens after a young person completes an intervention? ↓ **SUSTAINABILITY** Can positive behavioural changes be maintained?
### Our emerging gap **The challenge may be less about creating another program and more about strengthening the pathway between existing interventions.**
--- # 07 — What We Still Need to Know ## Our research is not finished ### We need stronger evidence about: **01** Which interventions have the strongest evidence of reducing reoffending? **02** Which young people benefit most from particular forms of intervention? **03** Where do young people fall out of existing support pathways? **04** What barriers prevent organisations from providing continuous support? **05** How do young people's own experiences differ from institutional assumptions?
### Evidence we will examine Academic literature · Government reports · Program evaluations · Reoffending data · Policy documents · Stakeholder perspectives --- # 08 — Our Proposed Contribution ## From fragmented intervention to a connected prevention pathway
### IDENTIFY ↓ ### ASSESS ↓ ### MATCH ↓ ### CONNECT ↓ ### FOLLOW UP

### Youth Reoffending Prevention Pathway **Early identification** → **Individual needs assessment** → **Matched mentoring / intervention** → **Education + employment + family support** → **Cross-service coordination** → **Longer-term follow-up**
### Our contribution We aim to develop an **evidence-informed framework** showing how existing services could be better connected to reduce the risk of repeated justice involvement.
